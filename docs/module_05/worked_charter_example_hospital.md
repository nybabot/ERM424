# Worked example: an engagement charter for a different sponsor

This is a completed charter for an invented hospital system, walked through at the week 5 sync. It shows the genre: one page, every field reasoned, rationale fields that argue rather than restate, success criteria that could fail, and an amendment log in use. It is not a template to copy: the sponsor, the data and the decision are different from yours, and nothing in it transfers to your engagement except the way of thinking.

| Analyst | J. Moreno |

## 1. Question and scope

How many patient-safety incidents that lead to a liability claim should the system expect in a year, how much do those claims cost when they close, and where across the five hospitals is that cost concentrated? In scope: incidents reported through the system's incident reporting platform from 2020 to 2024 at the five acute-care hospitals, and the closed liability claims linked to them, in counts per month and in dollars paid. Out of scope: the two outpatient clinics acquired in 2023, near misses that reached no patient, and any claim still open.

**Scope rationale.** The renewal turns on expected claim cost, so the scope follows the money: incidents that produced a claim and claims that have closed, where the paid amount is final. I considered including near misses, since they show where harm could occur, but their reporting rate changed when the platform was replaced in 2022 and they carry no dollar amount, so they would answer a different question. The clinics have fourteen months of reports and a different case mix; folding them in would blend two populations. Open claims are excluded from cost because their reserves are estimates; they stay in the counts.

## 2. Stakeholders

The CRO, who signs the submission to the insurer; the system's insurance broker, who negotiates the renewal on the CRO's behalf; the quality and patient-safety committee of the board, which receives the analysis for oversight.

## 3. Success criteria

1. A range for next year's count of claim-producing incidents, system-wide and per hospital, with the assumption it rests on stated, that the first quarter of 2026 can confirm or contradict.
2. A statement of how much of the five years' paid cost sits in the largest 5% of claims, with the number, so the broker can argue for the right retention level.
3. Every data limitation that changes either number named in the fitness memo, with its direction (overstates or understates).

**Success criteria rationale.** The insurer prices on expected cost and on the size of the largest claims, so criteria 1 and 2 are the two numbers the renewal needs, and each can fail: criterion 1 fails if the first quarter of 2026 falls outside the range for a reason the stated assumption did not allow; criterion 2 fails if the concentration figure moves materially when the two oldest years are dropped. Criterion 3 exists because the CRO signs: a limitation found by the insurer before we found it costs more than the limitation itself.

## 4. Constraints and exclusions

No access to patient records beyond the incident platform's fields; claim amounts only as recorded by the legal department; four weeks to the broker's deadline. The analysis will not attribute an incident to an individual clinician, and it will not estimate the cost of open claims.

## 5. Data required

The incident platform export, 2020 to 2024 (one row per incident; the harm level, the unit and hospital, the dates of occurrence and report); the legal department's closed-claims register (paid amount, close date, linked incident number); monthly inpatient days by hospital, so counts can be compared per patient day. Known limit: the platform was replaced in July 2022 and the harm-level scale changed with it; the fitness memo addresses how the two scales map.
